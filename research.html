Repeats & transposable elements · Published review

How mobile DNA helps insects adapt

Transposable elements can move to new places in a genome. In our review, Cheng Sun and I examined cases where this activity changes a trait that matters to the insect, from surviving an insecticide to developing a different wing pattern.

We traced four routes by which active elements contribute to adaptation. They can alter gene expression, rearrange chromosomes, change epigenetic regulation or move between species. The figures below connect these mechanisms to examples from insects.

Repeats & transposable elements · Master’s thesis in progress

Transposon-derived genes in the bumblebee brain

We combine single-cell, bulk and long-read RNA sequencing with evolutionary analyses to understand how transposable elements become host genes. We have identified domesticated transposon-derived genes that may play regulatory roles in bumblebee neurons.

Bioinformatics platforms · Live database

iBumblebee

iBumblebee brings together genomes, transcriptomes, population data and epigenomic resources across Bombus species. Researchers can browse assemblies, examine gene expression and compare species within a single resource.

I led the database project, designing its architecture and interface and building the data processing workflows. My work spans data curation and quality control; annotation of protein-coding genes, non-coding RNAs and repeats; and comparative analyses of orthologs, synteny and positive selection. I also integrated JBrowse2 and SynVisio for genome exploration.

Repeat variation · Population genomics

Short tandem repeats and local adaptation

Our study connects short tandem repeat length variation to gene expression and environmental adaptation in Bombus pyrosoma. Combining a telomere-to-telomere genome assembly with population resequencing and RNA-seq, the team identified expression-associated repeats and tested their effects on promoter activity and cold tolerance.

I contributed to genome quality assessment, annotation, exploratory co-expression analysis and field sampling. I also helped design and interpret regression, Pearson correlation and Bayenv2 analyses connecting repeat length with expression and environmental variation.

FusionBERT-CNN

2024 Greater Bay Area Cup · AI for Science

I developed FusionBERT-CNN to classify genes into high- and low-expression groups in the human GM12878 cell line. I combined promoter sequences with mRNA half-life features, then extended the model with chromatin accessibility data.

  1. Prepare the inputs

    I extracted a 2 kb promoter window centered on the transcription start site and paired it with mRNA half-life features.

  2. Encode the sequence

    I used DNABERT-2 embeddings and convolutional layers to learn a compact representation of the promoter.

  3. Fuse the modalities

    I combined sequence and half-life features for classification, then added eight chromatin accessibility features in the extended model.

  4. Inspect the predictions

    I compared attention-weighted sequence tokens and their positions in correctly classified high- and low-expression samples.

Interpretability analysis

I used attention analysis to identify DNA sequence tokens associated with low expression. These tokens occur throughout low-expression sequences, but are depleted around the transcription start site in high-expression genes. The contrast is visible as a trough in the promoter region of the high-expression group.
